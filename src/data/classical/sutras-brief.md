Use the eight limbs as a connected discipline, in this order: yama (ethical restraint), niyama (personal observance), asana (posture), pranayama (breath cultivation), pratyahara (sensory withdrawal), dharana (concentration), dhyana (meditation), and samadhi (absorption). Posture belongs within this wider practice. The restraints concern nonviolence, truthfulness, nonstealing, continence, and nonpossessiveness; the observances concern cleanliness, contentment, disciplined effort, study, and devotion to Isvara. (II.29–32)

Let steadiness and ease inform how you inhabit a posture. Refine effort by releasing needless exertion, and let attention open toward the boundless rather than making achievement the entire concern. The tradition holds that this cultivation leaves a practitioner less troubled by opposing conditions. These are principles for practice, not evidence of a particular physical mechanism or a reason to ignore pain. (II.46–48)

Pranayama follows the establishment of asana. The text considers outward, inward, and suspended breath, attended to through place, duration, and count, as well as a fourth mode beyond those divisions. The tradition holds that breath practice reduces what obscures clarity and makes attention more ready for concentration. This account supplies an order of study, not a breathing prescription or a claim about physiology. (II.49–53)

Pratyahara means loosening the senses' involvement with their usual objects so that they follow the mind's orientation. The tradition holds that this develops mastery of sensing. From there, dharana locates attention, dhyana sustains its continuity, and samadhi allows the contemplated object to predominate. These three together constitute samyama. (II.54–55; III.1–4)

Practice addresses the kleshas: misapprehension, identification with the instruments of knowing, attraction, aversion, and clinging to continued existence. Discipline, study, and devotion help wear these patterns down while preparing for absorption; meditation addresses their active expressions. Use this framework for noticing habits without treating a difficult sensation or emotion as personal failure. (II.1–11)

Keep practice and nonattachment together. Repeatedly returning to steadiness gives practice its direction; sustained care over time gives it a foundation. Nonattachment loosens the demand for experiences and rewards, including anticipated ones. The deeper relinquishment described here rests on discerning awareness from nature. For coaching, invite consistent participation without making an impressive posture the measure of progress. (I.12–16)
